EXERCISE 14

Statistical power and panel size

How many assessors are needed? Power, sample size for discrimination tests, the t-test and ANOVA.

Script (SK): cvicenie14.RScript (EN): exercise14.R🎓 Slides

What you will learn

  • what statistical power is and why “no difference shown” often just means a small panel,
  • to compute the required number of assessors for discrimination tests (sensR),
  • to plan the sample size for a t-test and ANOVA (power.t.test, pwr).

Power is the probability that a test detects a difference that really exists. We usually plan for 80% power (β = 0.2), for costly decisions 90%. Power grows with the size of the difference, the number of assessors and the sensitivity of the method.

Power in discrimination tests

d.primeSS(1, target.power = 0.80, method = "triangle")  # required n
d.primePwr(1, sample.size = 100, method = "triangle")   # power for a given n
discrimSS(pdA = 0.2, target.power = 0.80, pGuess = 1/3) # specified via pd instead of d′

Answer to the question from exercise 5a

In the paired preference test, 34 of 60 consumers chose sample A (57%), p = 0.37. If the true preference really is 57%:

  • the power of the test with 60 people is only about 26% – a non-significant result was almost certain,
  • about 350 consumers are needed for 80% power.
Why not 236?Exercise 5a said that at 57% the result would become significant from about 236 respondents. That is the number at which exactly 57% crosses the significance limit – but the real result fluctuates, so such a test would succeed only about half the time. Planning for 80% power gives a larger, safer number.

Scale data: t-test and ANOVA

For continuous ratings we need the effect size: the difference we want to detect (δ) and the standard deviation (σ) – from a pilot test or past measurements.

n ≈ 2 (z1−α/2 + z1−β)² σ² / δ²   (two independent groups) With δ = 0.5 and σ = 1: two independent groups need 64 people per group, a paired design only 34 assessors in total.

Plan power before the experimentComputing “power after the test” from the observed difference (post-hoc power) adds nothing – it is just a transformation of the p-value. The meaningful question is: what is the smallest difference that matters in practice, and how many assessors are needed to detect it reliably?
