The logic of hypothesis testing

Every test compares two statements. The null hypothesis H0: there is no difference between the samples (assessors are only guessing). The alternative H1: a difference exists. The test returns a p-value – the probability that, if H0 were true, we would get a result at least as extreme as the observed one.

p < 0.05 → reject H0 (the difference is statistically significant) p ≥ 0.05 does not mean “the samples are the same”, only “the difference could not be shown” – often because there were too few assessors.

Checking normality

Parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA) assume that the data come from a normal distribution. We check it with a combination of a plot and a test:

The Shapiro-Wilk test (shapiro.test) has H0: “the data are normal”. The logic is the reverse of what we are used to: p ≥ 0.05 is good news (normality is not rejected). Sensory scales with few distinct values (e.g. 6.9 – 7.5) often violate normality – always look at the box plot and QQ plot as well.

Binomial test in discrimination tests

In a paired (duo) or triangle test every assessor has two outcomes: correct / incorrect. If everyone were only guessing, the number of “correct” answers would follow a binomial distribution with the guessing probability p:

duo-trio1 + 21/2binom.test(x, n, p = 0.5)
triangle31/3binom.test(x, n, p = 1/3, alternative = "greater")
Task 2 – triangle vs. paired test42 correct out of 100 triangles: p = 0.043 (against 1/3) → the difference is shown. The same number in a paired test (against 1/2) would be below chance level. The triangle is “stricter” on guessing, so a smaller share of correct answers is enough.

χ² goodness-of-fit test

Compares observed counts O in categories with expected counts E (under H0, spread evenly):

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E,   df = k − 1

For the Likert questions about goulash: question 1 (p = 0.12) – opinions are spread randomly; questions 2 and 3 (p < 0.001) – respondents clearly agree. The expected count in every category should be at least 5.
