EXERCISE 21

Sample codes and the Williams design of serving order

How to prepare a serving plan: random three-digit codes and an order balanced for position and for the preceding sample.

Script (SK): cvicenie21.RScript (EN): exercise21.R

What you will learn

  • to explain why samples are coded and why the serving order matters,
  • to construct a Williams design for an even and an odd number of samples,
  • to check the balance of a plan with a position table and a carry-over table.

Why codes and why order

An assessor should know nothing about a sample except what they perceive. A label such as “A” or “1” suggests an order or quality, so samples are labelled with random three-digit codes. The second source of bias is the order in which the samples arrive:

EffectWhat it causesRemedy
expectationthe label hints which sample should be “better”random three-digit codes
positionthe first sample tends to be rated differently from the last (hunger, fatigue, adaptation)every sample equally often in every position
carry-overperception is influenced by the previous sample – after a very bitter one the next seems less bitterevery sample equally often after every other

A completely random order evens these effects out only on average and with a large panel. With 10 – 20 assessors it is better to balance the order.

The Williams design

The Williams design (Williams, 1949) is a Latin square with an extra property: every sample follows every other sample equally often. It therefore balances both position and the first-order carry-over effect.

first order: 0, 1, k − 1, 2, k − 2, …  others: + 1 (mod k) k – number of samples; for odd k the mirror image of the square is added (every order reversed)
Number of samples kNumber of ordersPanel size for full balance
366, 12, 18, …
444, 8, 12, …
51010, 20, 30, …
666, 12, 18, …

Checking the balance

A plan is checked with two tables: sample × position and previous × following sample. In a balanced plan all cells are equal.

carry_over(orders)               # Williams design, 4 orders

        following
previous A B C D
       A 0 1 1 1
       B 1 0 1 1
       C 1 1 0 1
       D 1 1 1 0

The same check for 12 assessors with a completely random order (part 4 of the script) shows what happens without balancing: sample D is in the first position only once but five times in the third and the fourth; D follows sample C six times, A only once.

Balance holds only for whole multiplesIf the number of assessors is not a multiple of the number of orders (or someone does not show up), the plan is only approximately balanced. Plan the panel size together with the design.
Too many samples?If an assessor cannot taste all the samples, a balanced incomplete block design is used – exercise 5d.

Task solutions

Click to expand a solution. Try the task on your own first – results were verified in R 4.5. The code continues from the variables in the script.

Task 1 – plan for 5 samples and 20 assessors 
samples <- c("A", "B", "C", "D", "E")
orders <- williams(5)
nrow(orders)
rows <- sample(rep(seq_len(nrow(orders)), length.out = 20))
table(rows)

For an odd number of samples the Williams design has 2 × 5 = 10 orders. With 20 assessors each one is used twice – the plan is fully balanced: every sample is in every position 4 times and every pair of neighbours occurs 4 times.

Task 2 – 6 samples, 15 assessors 
samples <- LETTERS[1:6]
orders <- williams(6)
rows <- sample(rep(1:6, length.out = 15))
p <- orders[rows, ]
table(sample = samples[p], position = col(p))
carry_over(p)

There are 6 orders for 6 samples. 15 is not a multiple of 6, so three orders are used 3 times and three only twice: the position and carry-over tables contain the values 2 and 3 instead of equal numbers. Full balance is reached with 12 or 18 assessors.

Task 3 – only 3 of 6 samples

If every assessor tastes only some of the samples, the complete block is replaced by a balanced incomplete block (BIB) design: every pair of samples meets at the same number of assessors. For 6 samples in blocks of 3 you need 10 blocks (assessors) or a multiple of 10; the data are analysed with the Durbin test (exercise 5d).
