CATA and napping

Rapid profiling methods with consumers: check-all-that-apply questions and projective mapping.

What you will learn

  • when to replace a classical descriptive profile with a rapid consumer method,
  • to analyse CATA questions with Cochran’s Q test and correspondence analysis,
  • to process napping (projective mapping) with multiple factor analysis (MFA).

Why rapid methods?

A classical descriptive profile (exercises 7 – 8, 15) requires months of panel training. Rapid methods use untrained consumers and deliver a result in a day. The price is lower precision and more assessors (40 – 100).

CATA – Check-All-That-Apply

For each sample the consumer gets a list of attributes and ticks all that apply. The result is a value of 0 or 1 for every consumer × product × attribute combination.

Cochran’s Q test

For each attribute it tests whether the share of ticks differs between products. It is the counterpart of McNemar’s test (exercise 5a) for more than two samples: the same consumers rate all products, so the consumer is a block (as in the Friedman test).

CochranQTest(cata$fruity ~ cata$product | cata$consumer)

CATA map

The table of counts products × attributes is a contingency table – so we display it with correspondence analysis (exercise 9). A product lies close to the attributes consumers assigned to it more often than to the others.

Attribute orderConsumers tick attributes at the top of the list more often. Randomise the order between consumers (but keep it the same for one consumer).

Napping – projective mapping

The assessor gets all samples at once and a sheet of paper (usually 60 × 40 cm). He/she places the samples so that similar ones are close and different ones far apart – by their own criteria. The X, Y coordinates of each sample are recorded.

Absolute coordinates cannot be compared between assessors – each rotates the map differently and uses the space differently. We compare configurations: MFA (multiple factor analysis) treats each assessor as a group of two variables (X, Y), balances their weight and finds a common consensus map.

MFA(napping.don, group = rep(2, n_assessors), type = rep("c", n_assessors))
MFA outputMeaning
choix = "ind"consensus map of samples
choix = "group"assessors near an axis share the consensus; assessors near the origin placed samples “their own way”
Type “c”, not “s”The coordinates are measured in centimetres on the same sheet, so we do not scale them (type = "c"). Distances in X and Y are equally important.
