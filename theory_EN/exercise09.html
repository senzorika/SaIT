EXERCISE 9

Correspondence analysis

A map of associations between the categories of a contingency table – products and target groups.

Script (SK): cvicenie9.RScript (EN): exercise9.R🎓 Slides

What you will learn

  • to write categorical data into a contingency table,
  • the principle of correspondence analysis (CA) – χ² distances and inertia,
  • to read a symmetric map of row and column categories.

Contingency table

Correspondence analysis works with counts: how many times a combination of two categories occurred. In the example – how many people from each target group chose product A, B or C.

If the choice of product did not depend on the group, every row would have the same distribution as the total row (38 : 26 : 36). CA looks for and displays the deviations from this independence – the same ones the χ² test measures.

From the table to the map

The total variability of the table is called inertia (= χ² / n). The dimensions of the map decompose it much like components in PCA; a table r × c has at most min(r, c) − 1 dimensions – here 2, so the map shows 100% of the inertia.

How to read the map

  • Centre of the map = the average profile. The farther from the centre, the more distinctive the category (the more it differs from the average).
  • A row near a column (in the same direction from the centre) = the group chooses the product more often than independence would imply.
  • Two rows close together = groups with similar preferences.
  • The exact distance between a group point and a product point in a symmetric map is not interpretable – look at the direction from the centre.
The shampoo taskGenerate a table of 8 shampoos × 5 groups (e.g. matrix(sample(10:90, 40, replace = TRUE), ncol = 5)), name the rows and columns, and for the nettle, birch and tropical shampoos find the hair colour lying in the same direction from the centre.
AssumptionsCA is a descriptive method without p-values. Whether the association is significant is decided by chisq.test(data). With very small counts (< 5), rare categories can “pull” the map to its edges.

Task 1 – shampoo packaging 
set.seed(9)
shampoos <- matrix(sample(10:90, 40, replace = TRUE), ncol = 5,
  dimnames = list(c("Birch","Nettle","Fruit","Egg","Nut","Chocolate","Menthol","Tropical"),
                  c("Blond","Brown","Black","Red","Men")))
chisq.test(shampoos)
biplot(corresp(shampoos, nf = 2))

The data are random; the result depends on set.seed(). With seed 9: the association is significant (χ² test p < 0.001), the first two dimensions explain 83% of the inertia. In the same direction from the map centre lie: nettle → men, birch → blond, tropical → black hair – those photos go on the packaging.
