Temporal methods – TDS and TCATA

How perception changes over time: dominance of sensations and temporal CATA curves.

Perception changes over time

With wine, beer, chocolate or chewing gum the perception develops: first sweetness, then sourness, at the end bitterness and a drying (astringent) feeling. A static profile (one number per attribute) blends this course into an average. Temporal methods record perception every second. Data are collected by the apps TDS_app.R and TCATA_app.R in the Senzometricke_appky/ folder.

TDS curves

At every time t we count how many assessors marked the attribute as dominant and divide by the number of assessors. The dominance rates of all attributes sum to 1 at every time point.

Ps = P0 + 1.645 · √[ P0 (1 − P0) / n ],   P0 = 1 / k k – number of attributes, n – number of evaluations (assessors × replicates). Limit according to Pineau et al. (2009) for a one-sided test at 0.05.
  • Chance level P0 – the dominance rate an attribute would reach if assessors chose randomly.
  • Significance limit – above it the attribute is dominant more often than chance would allow.
  • When reading, follow the sequence of dominant sensations and their duration (width above the limit).

TCATA curves and comparing products

In TCATA an assessor may cite several attributes at one moment, so the proportions do not sum to 1. We draw the curves of two products in one plot and at every time point test whether the citation proportion differs – e.g. with Fisher’s test on a 2 × 2 table.

Multiple testingA test every second means dozens of tests – a few “significant” points arise by chance. Consider a difference significant only over a continuous interval of several seconds, not isolated points.
Recording lengthAssessors swallow and the perception fades at different rates. With real data time is often standardised (0 = first selection, 1 = end of that assessor’s recording) so that curves can be averaged. The tempR package offers ready-made functions for standardisation and plotting.

Task 1 – when sweetness is dominant 
above <- dominance > limit
sapply(as.data.frame(above), function(x) range(time[x]))

The significance limit is 0.32. Sweetness is significantly dominant during 0 – 13 s, then fruitiness (16 – 33 s) and sourness (8 – 28 s) follow. Bitterness takes over at about 33 s (33 – 53 s) and astringency prevails at the end (38 – 60 s). These are simulated data; with real data the intervals will be shorter and less regular.
