EXERCISE 25

Sensory claims – superiority and parity

What a test must show for the claims “tastes better” or “tastes the same” to stand: the preference test, the equivalence test and an attribute claim.

Script (SK): cvicenie25.RScript (EN): exercise25.R

What you will learn

  • to distinguish superiority, parity and attribute claims and assign the right hypothesis to each,
  • to evaluate a paired preference test including “no preference” answers,
  • to establish parity with an equivalence test and determine the number of consumers needed.

Types of claims and their hypotheses

A sensory claim on a label or in an advertisement must be backed by a test. What matters is what is claimed – the null hypothesis depends on it:

ClaimWhat has to be shownTest
superiority – “consumers prefer A”share preferring A > 50 %two-sided binomial test
parity – “liked as much as B”the share lies in a narrow band around 50 %equivalence test
unsurpassed – “none tastes better”the share of A is not below 50 % − marginone-sided test
attribute – “intense flavour”mean of the descriptor ≥ a set limitconfidence interval, t-test

Superiority

Out of 300 consumers, 172 preferred product A (57.3 %). The two-sided binomial test gives p = 0.013 and a 95 % confidence interval of 51.5 – 63.0 % → the claim “preferred to B” is statistically supported.

“No preference” answersIf they are allowed, their handling must be fixed before the test. With 150 : 110 and 40 “no preference” answers, dropping them gives p = 0.015 (150 of 260), splitting them equally gives p = 0.024 (170 of 300). Choosing the method after seeing the result is not acceptable.

Parity – the equivalence test

A non-significant difference does not prove parity – the panel may simply have been small. Parity has to be established actively: the (1 − 2α) confidence interval of the share must lie entirely within a pre-set band of 50 % ± margin (here ± 10 percentage points, α = 0.05, i.e. a 90 % interval).

With a margin of ± 10 pp about 220 consumers are needed for 80 % power, with a stricter margin of ± 5 pp about 880.

Attribute claim

Let us define the claim “intense flavour” as a mean of at least 7 on a 10-point scale. A panel of 12 assessors gave a mean of 7.5 with a standard deviation of 1.2:

95 % CI = 7.5 ± 2.201 · 1.2 / √12 = 6.74 – 8.26 the lower limit 6.74 < 7 → the claim is not supported although the mean exceeds the limit

A one-sided t-test against 7 gives p = 0.088. About 38 ratings would be needed to separate a mean of 7.5 from the limit of 7 with 80 % power.

Statistics are not enoughA claim must rest on a representative sample of target consumers, current products from the market and a protocol written in advance (parity margin, α, handling of “no preference” answers). The legal framework and the methodological standards are summarised in the SAP chapter on sensory claims (in Slovak).

Task 1 – 140 of 250 prefer A 
binom.test(140, 250, p = 0.5)
parity(140, 250)

The share is 56 %, p = 0.066 → superiority is not supported. The 90 % confidence interval of 50.6 – 61.3 % exceeds the upper limit of the band (60 %) → parity is not supported either. The result is inconclusive: the test is sufficient for neither claim.

Task 2 – number of consumers for parity 
sapply(c(200, 214, 230), parity_power)
sapply(c(860, 880, 900), parity_power, margin = 0.05)

With a margin of ± 10 pp the power exceeds 80 % at about 220 consumers (for a binomial distribution the power fluctuates slightly with n; it first reaches 80 % at n = 214). With a margin of ± 5 pp about 880 are needed – half the margin means four times the sample.

Task 3 – why a non-significant difference is not enough 
parity(31, 60)

The SAP calculator gives the same result for 172 : 128 (p = 0.013; parity not supported). A non-significant result only means “a difference could not be shown” – with 60 consumers even 60 : 40 would come out non-significant. The claim “tastes the same” therefore requires an equivalence test with a pre-set margin and a sufficiently large sample.
