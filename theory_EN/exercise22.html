EXERCISE 22

Detection threshold – 3-AFC and the BET method

At which concentration does the panel detect a substance? An ascending series of 3-AFC tests, individual and group thresholds.

Script (SK): cvicenie22.RScript (EN): exercise22.R

What you will learn

  • to distinguish the detection and the recognition threshold and explain why a threshold is a probabilistic concept,
  • to compute the individual and the group threshold by the best-estimate (BET) method,
  • to estimate a threshold from the psychometric function with a correction for guessing.

What a threshold is

The detection threshold is the lowest concentration at which an assessor can tell the sample from water – not necessarily being able to say what they perceive. The recognition threshold is the higher concentration at which the taste or odour can also be named. A threshold is not a sharp boundary: at the same concentration an assessor detects the substance once and misses it another time. It is therefore defined by a probability – the concentration detected in 50 % of trials (or by 50 % of the panel).

The 3-AFC test in an ascending series

At each concentration the assessor receives three samples: two with water and one with the substance, and must pick the one with the substance (3-AFC, three-alternative forced choice). Without any perception the guess is right with probability 1/3. The concentrations rise geometrically, here in steps of 2: 0.5 – 1 – 2 – 4 – 8 – 16 g/l.

Best Estimate Threshold (BET)

The individual threshold lies between the highest missed concentration and the next higher one. Their geometric mean is taken as the best estimate:

The group threshold is 2.52 g/l; a standard deviation of 0.52 of the log10 thresholds means that the individual thresholds differ by more than an order of magnitude (0.35 to 11.3 g/l).

Boundary casesAn assessor who was correct at all concentrations (A4) gets an estimate half a step below the series; one who missed at the highest concentration, half a step above it. In both cases the true threshold is unknown – the series has to be extended.

The psychometric function

If many people are tested at each concentration, the threshold is estimated from the proportion of correct answers. One third of the assessors guess correctly without perceiving anything, so the proportion correct does not start at zero but at 1/3:

P(correct) = 1/3 + 2/3 · P(detection),  P(detection) = 1 / (1 + e−(a + b·log₂ c)) threshold = the concentration at which P(detection) = 0.5, i.e. 2/3 correct answers
Why not 50 % correct?In a 3-AFC test, 50 % correct answers mean only 25 % detection (0.5 = 1/3 + 2/3 · 0.25). Without the correction for guessing the threshold would be underestimated.

Task 1 – sensitivity of the assessors 
round(sort(thresholds), 2)

The most sensitive assessor is A4 (0.35 g/l), the least sensitive A5 (11.3 g/l). The estimate for A4 is unreliable – they were correct everywhere, so their threshold lies somewhere below 0.5 g/l; the series should be extended downwards (0.25; 0.125 g/l). The answers of A5 are inconsistent and the test would be worth repeating.

Task 2 – why the correct answer at 2 g/l does not count 
(1 / 3)^3

After the correct answer at 2 g/l, A5 missed twice (4 and 8 g/l) – so it was probably a lucky guess. BET takes the last miss, not the first correct answer. An assessor who perceives nothing guesses three 3-AFC tests in a row with probability (1/3)³ = 3.7 %; a single test as often as 33 %.

Task 3 – BET versus the psychometric function

The SAP calculator gives the same individual thresholds and the group threshold of 2.52 g/l. The psychometric function gave 3.77 g/l – but these are different data (30 assessors) and a different definition: BET averages individual thresholds, the model looks for the concentration with 50 % detection in the whole group. With few assessors BET depends strongly on individuals (here A4 and A5).
