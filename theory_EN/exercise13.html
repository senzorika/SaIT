EXERCISE 13

Thurstonian model and d′

Discrimination tests revisited: d′ as a method-independent measure of difference, psychometric functions and the similarity test.

Script (SK): cvicenie13.RScript (EN): exercise13.R🎓 Slides

What you will learn

  • why the proportion of correct answers cannot be compared across discrimination tests,
  • what Thurstonian d′ expresses and how to compute it with sensR,
  • when to use a difference test and when a similarity test.

The problem: 42% is not always 42%

In exercise 5a the binomial test told us whether samples differ. It did not tell us how much. The proportion correct pc cannot answer that, because it depends on the method: the same difference between samples gives 76% correct answers in a 2-AFC test but only 42% in a triangle test. We need a measure that describes the samples, not the test.

The Thurstonian model

Thurstone (1927) assumed that the perceived intensity varies from tasting to tasting around a mean value according to a normal distribution. Two samples are two distributions; their distance measured in standard deviations is d′ (d-prime).

d′interpretation (rule of thumb)
< 0.5difference practically imperceptible
≈ 1small, but a trained panel detects it
≥ 2clear difference, noticed even by ordinary consumers

Psychometric functions – the bridge between d′ and pc

Every method uses a different decision strategy and therefore has a different function pc = f(d′). psyfun() computes it, psyinv() goes the other way.

Task 1 – comparing two testsTriangle 42/100 gives d′ ≈ 1.0. Duo-trio 36/50 (72%) looks similarly “successful”, but corresponds to d′ ≈ 1.8 – the second product differs from the control almost twice as much. Without d′ we would not see this from pc.

Difference test vs. similarity test

When changing a supplier or reducing sugar we want to show the opposite: that the samples are similar enough. Then we swap the hypotheses – H0: the difference is at least pd0; H1: it is smaller.

discrim(38, 100, method = "triangle", test = "similarity", pd0 = 0.2)
No difference shown ≠ similarityA non-significant difference test (p ≥ 0.05) does not mean the samples are the same – the panel may simply have been small. Similarity must be shown with a similarity test and a limit pd0 chosen in advance (e.g. 0.2 – at most 20% discriminators).
