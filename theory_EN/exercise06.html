EXERCISE 6

Correlation and linear regression

Strength and direction of the relationship between instrumental and sensory attributes, and a simple predictive model.

Script (SK): cvicenie6.RScript (EN): exercise6.R🎓 Slides

What you will learn

  • to express the strength and direction of a relationship with a correlation coefficient,
  • to tell the Pearson, Spearman and Kendall coefficients apart,
  • to build and read a simple linear regression model.

Correlation – direction and strength

The correlation coefficient r ranges from −1 to +1. The sign gives the direction (as one attribute grows, the other grows / falls), the absolute value the strength of the relationship.

CoefficientMeasuresWhen to use
Pearson method="pearson"linear relationshipcontinuous, roughly normal data without outliers
Spearman method="spearman"monotonic relationship (on ranks)ordinal scales, outliers, a non-linear but still increasing relationship
Kendall τ method="kendall"agreement of pair rankingssmall samples, many tied values

Correlation is not everything

Correlation ≠ causationA high correlation between two sensory attributes can arise because both depend on a third factor (e.g. fat content), or from the halo effect – an assessor who likes the product rates all attributes higher.

Simple linear regression

Regression goes one step further: it looks for a line with which we predict the dependent variable y (a sensory attribute) from the independent x (e.g. the amount of sugar or an instrumental measurement). The least squares method chooses the line so that the sum of squared residuals (vertical distances of the points from the line) is the smallest.

ŷ = b₁ + b₂ · x b₁ – intercept, b₂ – slope: how much y changes when x increases by 1

How to read summary(lm(y ~ x))

Part of the outputMeaning
Estimatecoefficients b₁ (Intercept) and b₂ (next to the name of x)
Pr(>|t|) for xp-value of the test whether the slope differs from zero (whether x helps at all)
Multiple R-squaredcoefficient of determination R² – share of the variability of y explained by the model (0 – 1)
Residual standard errortypical prediction error in units of y
Order in the formulalm(y ~ x) – what we predict is on the left. lm(sweetness ~ sugar_amount) predicts sweetness from sugar; the reversed form would give a different line and a different meaning. Predicting outside the range of measured x (extrapolation) is risky.

Task 1 – correlations of instrumental and sensory attributes 
texture <- data.frame(tm_hardness, tm_firmness, tm_conductivity, se_stickiness,
                      se_juiciness, se_swallowability, se_chewiness)
r <- cor(texture, method = "spearman")
round(r, 2)
sort(round(r[, "se_juiciness"], 2))

Strongest negative correlation: tm_conductivity × se_chewiness, ρ = −0.72 (and se_swallowability × se_chewiness, −0.61). Juiciness correlates most with tm_firmness (ρ = 0.75), then with tm_hardness (0.53).

Task 2 – predicting juiciness 
model <- lm(se_juiciness ~ tm_firmness)
summary(model)
predict(model, newdata = data.frame(tm_firmness = 4))

juiciness = 0.90 + 0.77 · firmness; slope p = 0.0003, R² = 0.60. Prediction is possible but rough – the model explains 60% of the variability and the typical error is about 1 point. On such a short scale (1 – 5) it gives only an indicative estimate.
