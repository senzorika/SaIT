EXERCISE 24

Conjoint analysis

How a consumer builds a preference from product features: part-worth utilities, attribute importance and a market simulation.

Script (SK): cvicenie24.RScript (EN): exercise24.R

What you will learn

  • to design product profiles from attributes and their levels,
  • to estimate part-worth utilities by regression and compute the relative importance of attributes,
  • to use individual utilities for segmentation and a simple market simulation.

The principle

Asked “is price important to you?”, almost everyone says “yes”. Conjoint analysis therefore does not ask about attributes one by one: the consumer rates whole product profiles (e.g. “full-fat strawberry yogurt for €0.79”) and the importance of the attributes is computed from how the rating changes with their levels.

rating = β₀ + ufat + uflavour + uprice + ε u – part-worth utility of a level; within one attribute the utilities sum to zero

In the script the yogurt has three attributes with 2, 3 and 3 levels, i.e. 2 × 3 × 3 = 18 profiles (a full factorial design). They are rated by 40 consumers on a 9-point scale.

Part-worth utilities and importance

importance of an attribute = range of its utilities / sum of the ranges of all attributes fat 1.26 · flavour 1.13 · price 2.18 → 28 % · 25 % · 48 %
Importance depends on the chosen levelsIf the price varied only between €0.75 and €0.79, its range and its “importance” would be small. The result holds only for the tested range of levels.

Individual utilities and segments

An average can hide groups with opposite preferences. Rating-based conjoint allows a model to be estimated for every respondent (18 ratings, 6 parameters) and their utilities to be clustered (exercise 8):

Market simulation

For every concept the respondent's part-worth utilities are added up; under the first-choice rule the respondent “buys” the concept with the highest utility.

ConceptCompositionShare of first choice
Light0.1 %, strawberry, €0.5950 % (the whole “price” segment)
Premium3.5 %, chocolate, €0.9930 %
Classic3.5 %, strawberry, €0.7920 %
What to watch out forThe number of profiles grows by multiplying the levels – 5 attributes with 3 levels each give 243 profiles; a fractional factorial design is then used. The first-choice rule is a simplification (it ignores availability and brand). In choice-based conjoint, where respondents choose among profiles instead of rating them, a multinomial logit model replaces the regression.

Task solutions

Click to expand a solution. Try the task on your own first – results were verified in R 4.5. The code continues from the variables in the script.

Task 1 – attribute importance in the clusters 
importance <- function(u) {
  r <- c(fat = diff(range(u[1:2])), flavour = diff(range(u[3:5])), price = diff(range(u[6:8])))
  round(100 * r / sum(r), 1)
}
t(apply(clusters$centers, 1, importance))

In the whole sample price is the most important attribute (48 %), followed by fat (28 %) and flavour (25 %). The clusters look different: in the “price” segment price has 68 %, in the “taste” segment fat (42 %) and flavour (37 %) lead and price has only 22 %. The average result describes neither segment well.

Task 2 – the best yogurt for each cluster 
round(clusters$centers, 2)

“Price” segment: 3.5 % fat, strawberry, €0.59. “Taste” segment: 3.5 % fat, chocolate, €0.59. Raising the price from €0.59 to €0.99 lowers the utility by 3.38 points in the “price” segment but only by 0.96 points in the “taste” segment – a premium chocolate yogurt can carry the price.
